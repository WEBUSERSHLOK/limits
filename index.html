Unit 1.1 Notebook: "What is a limit?"

What is a Limit? The Intended Height

In calculus, a limit is the intended height ($y$-value) that a function approaches as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to a target number $c$, regardless of whether the function actually reaches that point or is even defined there!

1. Left & Right Disagree

$$\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) \ne \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x)$$ The graph jumps. For example, $f(x) = \frac{|x|}{x}$ gives $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right.

2. Unbounded (Shoots to $\pm\infty$)

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^2} = \infty$$ The $y$-values grow infinitely large, so they never approach a single real finite number $L$.

3. Oscillating Wildly

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$$ As $x \to 0$, the angle $\frac{1}{x}$ blows up to $\infty$, causing the sine wave to bounce between $-1$ and $+1$ infinite times.

Key Notebook Takeaway: $f(c)$ is where the point is; $\lim_{x\to c} f(x)$ is where the curve is aiming!
Unit 1.2 Notebook: "Basic Limit Properties"

Rule #1: Always Try "Plug & Chug" First

For polynomials, radical roots, rational functions (with non-zero denominator), and trig functions within their domains: Direct substitution always works!

Algebraic Properties:
  • • Scalar: $\lim [b \cdot f(x)] = b \cdot L$
  • • Sum / Diff: $\lim [f(x) \pm g(x)] = L \pm K$
  • • Product: $\lim [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot K$
  • • Quotient: $\lim \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{K}$ (provided $K \ne 0$)
  • • Power: $\lim [f(x)]^n = L^n$
Trig Limits by Direct Sub:

If $c$ is in the domain, just substitute:

$\lim_{x \to \pi/4} \tan x = \tan(\pi/4) = 1$
$\lim_{x \to \pi/2} (x \sin x) = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{\pi}{2}$

Only when direct substitution yields $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{k}{0}$ do you need advanced techniques!

Unit 1.3 Notebook: "Indeterminate Form"

When Substitution Gives $\frac{0}{0}$: The 3 Big Fixes

$\frac{0}{0}$ is NOT undefined, nor is it $0$ or $1$. It is an indeterminate form. It signals a hidden hole that cancels out with algebra!

1. Factoring & Canceling

Used for polynomials:

$$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-5x+6}{x^2+3x-10}$$ $$= \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x-2)(x-3)}{(x-2)(x+5)} = \frac{-1}{7}$$
2. Conjugate Rationalizing

Used when radicals cause $\frac{0}{0}$:

$$\lim_{x \to 9} \frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x}+3}{\sqrt{x}+3}$$ $$= \lim_{x \to 9} \frac{x-9}{(x-9)(\sqrt{x}+3)} = \frac{1}{6}$$
3. Complex Fractions

Multiply top & bottom by the common denominator:

$$\lim_{x \to -3} \frac{\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{3}}{x+3} \cdot \frac{3x}{3x}$$ $$= \lim_{x \to -3} \frac{3+x}{(x+3)(3x)} = -\frac{1}{9}$$
Unit 1.4 Notebook: "Continuity & One-Sided Limits"

Continuity & The 3-Part Checklist

A function $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$ if and only if all three conditions hold:

Condition 1
$f(c)$ exists

The point is defined on the graph (not a hole with no point, and not an asymptote).

Condition 2
$\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ exists

The left-hand limit equals the right-hand limit: $\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x)$.

Condition 3
$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$

The point is located in the exact spot the curve was aiming for!

Solving for $c$ in Piecewise Functions: Set the left rule equal to the right rule at the boundary $x = x_0$ to guarantee that $\lim_{x \to x_0^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0^+} f(x)$.
Unit 1.5 Notebook: "Infinite Limits"

Infinite Limits: When Substitution Gives $\frac{\text{Non-Zero}}{0}$

If direct substitution yields $\frac{\text{number}}{0}$ where the numerator is NOT zero, you have a Vertical Asymptote. The limit is either $+\infty$, $-\infty$, or DNE (if the two sides differ in sign)!

Notebook Example Decoded:
$$\lim_{x \to 2^-} \frac{3x+1}{x-2} = \frac{7}{0^-}$$

Sign Analysis: As $x \to 2^-$ (e.g., $x = 1.99$), the numerator is $3(1.99)+1 \approx +6.97 > 0$, but the denominator is $1.99 - 2 = -0.01 < 0$.

$$\frac{\text{Positive}}{\text{Tiny Negative}} \implies -\infty$$

Right-Side Check:
$$\lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{3x+1}{x-2} = \frac{7}{0^+}$$

As $x \to 2^+$ (e.g., $x = 2.01$), the numerator is positive and the denominator is $2.01 - 2 = +0.01 > 0$.

$$\frac{\text{Positive}}{\text{Tiny Positive}} \implies +\infty$$

Because left is $-\infty$ and right is $+\infty$, the overall two-sided $\lim_{x\to 2} f(x)$ does not exist (DNE).

Unit 1.6 Notebook: "Limits at Infinity"

Limits at Infinity: End Behavior & Horizontal Asymptotes

Finding $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)$ or $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x)$ asks: "What horizontal ceiling or floor does the curve flatten out against as $x$ heads forever right or left?"

Case 1: Degree Num < Den
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{4x^3}{x^4+1} = 0$$

The denominator grows much faster, driving the fraction to 0. Horizontal asymptote at $y = 0$.

Case 2: Degree Num = Den
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^2-4x}{2x^2+9} = \frac{1}{2}$$

The powers tie! The limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients ($\frac{1}{2}$). HA at $y = \frac{1}{2}$.

Case 3: Degree Num > Den
$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^3+4x}{x^2+1} = \text{DNE} \ (\infty)$$

The numerator dominates. The curve blows up without bound, so no horizontal asymptote exists.

Teacher's Grading Warning from Notebook: On tests, if asked for a Horizontal Asymptote, you can state the rule directly. But if asked to evaluate the limit, divide every term by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator to show complete work!
Unit 1.7 Notebook: "Squeeze Theorem"

The Squeeze Theorem & Essential Trig Limits

If $h(x) \le f(x) \le g(x)$ and both bounding functions squeeze into the same value: $\lim h(x) = L$ and $\lim g(x) = L$, then the middle function $f(x)$ is forced to have that same limit $L$!

Two Fundamental Trig Limits:
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$$
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$$

Derived directly from geometric squeeze theorems. Memorize these cold!

The Scaling Trick $(\sin kx)$:

To evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(3x)}{x}$, balance with $\frac{3}{3}$:

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \left(3 \cdot \frac{\sin 3x}{3x}\right) = 3 \cdot (1) = 3$$

Similarly, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 5x \sin 4x}{x^2} = 5 \cdot 4 = 20$.

Unit 1.8 Notebook: "Intermediate Value Theorem"

The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, it must take on every single $y$-value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ at least once!

The 2 Non-Negotiable Conditions:
  1. $f(x)$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$.
  2. $k$ is strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.

Conclusion: There exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = k$.

Most Common AP Application: Proving Roots

If $f(a) < 0$ (negative) and $f(b) > 0$ (positive), because $0$ lies between a negative and a positive, $f(x) = 0$ must have at least one solution (root) in $(a, b)$!
